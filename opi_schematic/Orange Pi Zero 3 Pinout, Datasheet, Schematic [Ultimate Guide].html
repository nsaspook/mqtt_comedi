Orange Pi Zero 3 Pinout, Datasheet, Schematic [Ultimate Guide]

November 22, 2026 by Khaled Magdy

In this guide, you’ll find the Orange Pi Zero 3 pinout diagram, its datasheet and schematic diagram, and every pin group explained with its default pins, the overlay that enables it. Without further ado, let’s get right into it!

The Orange Pi Zero 3 is a tiny 50 x 55mm Linux board built around the Allwinner H618 (4x Cortex-A53 at 1.5GHz) with 1GB, 1.5GB, 2GB, or 4GB of LPDDR4, Gigabit Ethernet, Wi-Fi 5, and Bluetooth 5.0. Its main header has 26 pins, not 40, with 17 GPIOs at 3.3v, and a few of them carry on-board pull-ups and boot straps that most pinout pages never mention.

Orange Pi Zero 3 Pinout Diagram

Here is a detailed Orange Pi Zero 3 Pinout Diagram with colored labels and legends to help you easily identify the functionalities that each pin can perform. Each GPIO shows its physical pin number, the Allwinner port name (PH5), the Linux GPIO number, the wiringOP (wPi) number, and every function the H618 can route to it.

Read it from the grey strip in the middle: odd pins (1, 3, 5…) are on the left, which is the outer row along the board edge, and even pins (2, 4, 6…) are on the right, the inner row. Pin 1 is the corner pin with the yellow housing next to the mounting hole. The plastic housing is colour-coded: yellow is 3.3v, red is 5v, black is GND, and blue is GPIO. Orange Pi Zero 3 13-Pin Header Pinout

The Orange Pi Zero 3 also has a single-row 13-pin function header next to the RJ45 jack. It carries two extra USB 2.0 host ports, the audio line out, TV-out (composite video), the IR receiver input, and four more GPIOs. Pin 1 (5v) is the end next to the Ethernet jack.

Click to expand the full pin table (13 pins)

There’s no microphone input on the Zero 3’s 13-pin header, and no VBUS switch for the two USB ports: power USB devices from pin 1 (5v) and pin 2 (GND). Orange Pi’s 13-pin expansion board turns these pins into two USB-A ports, a 3.5mm audio/TV jack, and an IR receiver, and with it fitted, pins 10–12 aren’t free any more.

Buy an Orange Pi Zero 3: If you don’t have one yet, you can get the official board from Amazon or AliExpress. Pick the RAM size that fits your project: 1GB is plenty for GPIO and sensor work, and 2GB or 4GB for a desktop, Docker, or a 3D-printer host.

Orange Pi Zero 3 Datasheet

The Orange Pi Zero 3 is built around the Allwinner H618 system-on-chip. Orange Pi doesn’t publish a separate board datasheet: the user manual is the datasheet, with the specs, both header tables, and the overlay list. Here are the official documents (Orange Pi hosts them on Google Drive, linked from its Orange Pi Zero 3 service and support page):

Which document do I need? The user manual for the pin tables, the overlay names and the Linux device nodes; the schematic for the power path, the on-board resistors and the boot straps; and the mechanical drawing for a case or a mount.

Note: Allwinner doesn’t publish an H618 datasheet or user manual, so there’s no official chip datasheet to link. The alternate functions in this guide come from the H618 ball names printed on Orange Pi’s schematic (sheet 1), cross-checked with the mainline Linux pin controller. The electrical limits further down are from Allwinner’s datasheet for the H616, the H618’s sibling chip with the same GPIO design, and they’re labelled as H616 values.

Official source: the Orange Pi Zero 3 schematic (PDF), OrangePi-Schematic of ZERO3, Rev 1.2, 13 July 2023. The watermark across the sheets is on Orange Pi’s own file. Here’s what to look at:

  • 5v pins (sheets 4 and 5): the USB-C connector’s VBUS pins go straight to the VCC-5V net, and header pins 2, 4 and 13-pin pin 1 sit on that same net. There’s no fuse, no reverse-polarity diode and no switch in between. Two 5.1kΩ resistors on CC1/CC2 make the board a plain 5v USB-C sink, so any charger gives it 5v and there’s no Power Delivery.
  • 3.3v pins (sheets 4 and 5): pins 1 and 17 are VCC3V3-EXT, one branch of the board’s 3.3v rail group. The same group also feeds the GPIO banks (VCC-IO, VCC-PC), the microSD card, the Ethernet PHY and the Wi-Fi module, and Orange Pi doesn’t state its current rating.
  • Pull-ups on the 26-pin header (sheet 5): R31/R30 (2kΩ) pull pins 3 and 5 (I2C3) up to 3.3v, and R28/R29 (2kΩ) pull pins 16 and 18 up to 3.3v on nets named GPIO-SDA and GPIO-SCK. All four pins idle high.
  • Boot straps (sheet 1): PC6 and PC5 are the H618’s BOOT_SEL4 and BOOT_SEL3 straps, with a 10kΩ pull-up (R20) and a 10kΩ pull-down (R19). They come out on header pins 11 and 13.
  • PMU interrupt (sheets 1 and 4): the AXP313A’s IRQ output (PMU_IRQ) is tied to PC9, header pin 7, through R42 (0Ω).
  • USB-A port (sheet 5): its VBUS goes through an SY6280 power switch, enabled by PC16 (USB1-DRVVBUS) with a 6.8kΩ current-limit resistor. The two USB ports on the 13-pin header have no such switch.
  • LEDs and debug UART (sheet 5): the red LED is on PC12 (1kΩ) and the green LED on PC13 (470Ω), both active high. The 3-pin debug header is GND, RX, TX (pins 1, 2, 3), wired to UART0 (PH1/PH0) through 100Ω resistors.

Orange Pi Zero 3 Specifications Summary

  • The Orange Pi Zero 3 has a 26-pin header (not 40 pins), so Raspberry Pi HATs don’t fit it.
  • It has 17 GPIOs on the 26-pin header and 4 more on the 13-pin header, all 3.3v and not 5v tolerant.
  • Current limit (H616 datasheet values): 6mA max per pin, 4mA by default.
  • Pins 3, 5, 16 and 18 have 2kΩ pull-ups on the board and always idle high.
  • Pins 11 and 13 are the H618’s boot straps (10kΩ pull-up / pull-down).
  • Power pins: 5v on pins 2 and 4 (USB-C VBUS, no fuse), 3.3v on pins 1 and 17, and 5 GND pins (6, 9, 14, 20, 25).
  • There are no analog inputs: add an external I2C ADC.
  • I2C: I2C3 on pin 3 (SDA) and pin 5 (SCL), /dev/i2c-3 on Orange Pi OS.
  • UART: UART5 on pin 8 (TX) and pin 10 (RX), /dev/ttyS5 on Orange Pi OS.
  • SPI: SPI1 on pins 19, 21 and 23, with CS1 on pin 24 → /dev/spidev1.1.
  • PWM: PWM1 (pin 10) and PWM2 (pin 8); PWM3 and PWM4 are on the debug UART pins.
  • Debug console: a separate 3-pin UART header at 115200 baud.

Orange Pi Zero 3 Pins to Use With Caution

Orange Pi Zero 3 Pins Functions

Only 17 of the 26 pins of the Orange Pi Zero 3 header are GPIO pins; the rest are power and ground. The pins are divided into the following groups based on the hardware functionality they’re used for.

  • Power Pins (2x 5v, 2x 3.3v, 5x GND)
  • 17x GPIO Pins (+4 on the 13-pin header, with 4 numbering systems)
  • Boot Strap Pins (pins 11 and 13)
  • Analog Input Pins (none on the headers)
  • UART Pins (UART5)
  • I2C Pins (I2C3)
  • SPI Pins (SPI1)
  • 2x PWM Pins (+2 on the debug UART)
  • Debug UART Pins (separate 3-pin header)

Every bus is off by default, and each one is turned on with an overlay. Pick your overlays first, so no two of them claim the same pin. We’ll discuss each pin group in the following sections one by one in more detail.

Orange Pi Zero 3 Power Pins

The Orange Pi Zero 3 26-pin header has two 5v pins (2 and 4), two 3.3v pins (1 and 17) and five GND pins (6, 9, 14, 20 and 25), plus 5v and GND on pins 1 and 2 of the 13-pin header. They’re highlighted in red and black in the pinout diagram, and in the header’s own red, yellow and black plastic.

5V pins: when you power the Orange Pi Zero 3 over USB-C, these pins give you that same 5v, so you can power modules and the 13-pin USB ports from them. They work the other way too: the user manual (section 2.9) allows powering the board through a 5v pin and GND, as long as the supply and the wires can deliver the current. The schematic shows nothing between these pins and the USB-C connector: no fuse, no reverse-polarity diode, no back-feed protection.

3.3V pins: a 3.3v output for sensors and logic chips. The same 3.3v rail group also feeds the GPIO banks, the microSD card, the Ethernet PHY and the Wi-Fi module, and Orange Pi doesn’t publish a current limit for it. Keep the load small (sensors and modules), and give anything heavier its own 3.3v regulator powered from 5v.

Power supply: the Orange Pi Zero 3 wants a good 5v / 2A or 5v / 3A supply into its USB-C port. The port has plain 5.1kΩ CC resistors and no Power Delivery, so a USB-C PD charger simply gives it 5v. The manual blames most continuous restarts during boot on the power supply or the USB-C cable, so try a better supply and a short, thick cable first.

Note: Never connect a supply to the 5v pins while the USB-C power cable is plugged in. They’re the same net, so the two supplies fight each other with nothing in between. Use one or the other, and if you power the board from the header, use a regulated 5v supply rated for at least 2A, with short, thick wires.

Never forget to connect the ground of every circuit to the Orange Pi Zero 3’s GND (common ground). External sensors and modules need the same ground line as the board to communicate with it properly.

Orange Pi Zero 3 GPIO Pins

The Orange Pi Zero 3 has 17 GPIO pins on its 26-pin header and 4 more (PC1, PI16, PI6, PH10) on the 13-pin header, and every one of them is a 3.3v pin that is not 5v tolerant. Each one can be an input or an output, or be switched to an alternate function (UART, I2C, SPI or PWM) with an overlay.

These limits are from Allwinner’s H616 datasheet (Revision 1.0), because Allwinner publishes no H618 datasheet. The H616 gives no total or per-bank current, so treat the pins as signal outputs: an LED with a 1kΩ resistor is fine, but anything that draws real current (relays, motors, LED strips, buzzers) needs a transistor or a driver board.

Orange Pi Zero 3 Pull-Up Resistors on Pins 3, 5, 16 and 18

Four pins of the Orange Pi Zero 3 header have strong 2kΩ pull-up resistors to 3.3v on the board: pins 3 and 5 (I2C3, R31/R30), and pins 16 and 18 (PC15/PC14, R28/R29). The manual lists pins 16 and 18 as plain GPIOs, but the schematic names their nets GPIO-SDA and GPIO-SCK. So:

  • These four pins always idle high. The H618’s internal 100kΩ pull-down can’t win against 2kΩ, so a pull-down setting does nothing on them.
  • They’re ideal active-low inputs. Wire a push button from pin 16 or 18 to GND, and it reads 1 when released and 0 when pressed, with no extra resistor.
  • Pins 16 and 18 make a ready-made software I2C pair (there’s no hardware I2C on PC14/PC15). Linux’s i2c-gpio driver can turn them into a second I2C bus.
  • As outputs they still work, but while one is low it sinks about 1.65mA through the pull-up, on top of your load.

Orange Pi Zero 3 GPIO Numbering: Physical vs Allwinner vs Linux vs wiringOP

Every GPIO on the Orange Pi Zero 3 has four names, and each tool wants a different one. The Raspberry Pi’s BCM numbers don’t apply here at all. Take physical pin 3 as an example:

The Linux number is built from the port name: port × 32 + pin, where PA = 0, PB = 1, PC = 2 … PH = 7 and PI = 8. PH5 is 7 × 32 + 5 = 229, PC11 (pin 12) is 2 × 32 + 11 = 75, and PI16 (13-pin pin 11) is 8 × 32 + 16 = 272. All the header GPIOs live on the H618’s main pin controller, which libgpiod sees as one chip, gpiochip0, so the line offset there is the same number.

wiringOP only knows the wPi numbers, and gpio readall prints them next to the physical pins. One oddity: it lists the four 13-pin GPIOs as “physical” pins 27, 29, 31 and 33 (wPi 17 to 20), and the newest wiringOP adds 28 and 30 for PWM3/PWM4 on the debug UART pins.

gpio readall              # wiringOP: physical, wPi, GPIO name and mode of every pin
gpioinfo gpiochip0         # libgpiod: list the lines of the main pin controller
gpioset gpiochip0 75=1     # libgpiod v1 tools: drive PC11 (pin 12) high

Orange Pi Zero 3 GPIO Code Example (C/C++ and Python)

Here’s the same job in both languages: blink an LED on pin 12 (PC11), a free GPIO with nothing else attached. Wire the LED’s anode to pin 12 through a 1kΩ resistor (about 1mA, well under the pin’s limit), and its cathode to GND on pin 14. Both use the libgpiod v2 API (gpiochip0, line 75): install libgpiod-dev on a release that packages v2 (Debian 13, Ubuntu 24.04) or build it from source, and pip install gpiod for the Python bindings. Images that ship libgpiod v1.6 need the older API.

// Blink an LED on pin 12 (PC11) with libgpiod v2
// Build: gcc blink.c -o blink -lgpiod
#include <gpiod.h>
#include <unistd.h>
 
int main(void)
{
    unsigned int line = 75;  /* PC11 = 2 x 32 + 11 */
    struct gpiod_chip *chip = gpiod_chip_open("/dev/gpiochip0");
    struct gpiod_line_settings *st = gpiod_line_settings_new();
    gpiod_line_settings_set_direction(st, GPIOD_LINE_DIRECTION_OUTPUT);
    struct gpiod_line_config *cfg = gpiod_line_config_new();
    gpiod_line_config_add_line_settings(cfg, &line, 1, st);
    struct gpiod_line_request *led = gpiod_chip_request_lines(chip, NULL, cfg);
 
    for (int i = 0; i < 10; i++) {
        gpiod_line_request_set_value(led, line, (i % 2) ? GPIOD_LINE_VALUE_INACTIVE
                                                        : GPIOD_LINE_VALUE_ACTIVE);
        usleep(500000);
    }
    gpiod_line_request_release(led);
    gpiod_chip_close(chip);
    return 0;
}

# Blink an LED on pin 12 (PC11) with the gpiod v2 bindings
# Run: sudo python3 blink.py
import time
import gpiod
from gpiod.line import Direction, Value
 
LINE = 75   # PC11 = 2 x 32 + 11, on gpiochip0
 
with gpiod.request_lines(
        "/dev/gpiochip0", consumer="blink",
        config={LINE: gpiod.LineSettings(direction=Direction.OUTPUT)}) as req:
    for _ in range(5):
        req.set_value(LINE, Value.ACTIVE)
        time.sleep(0.5)
        req.set_value(LINE, Value.INACTIVE)
        time.sleep(0.5)

With wiringOP (preinstalled on Orange Pi OS) the same LED is gpio mode 6 out and gpio write 6 1 in the terminal, because pin 12 is wPi 6. In C, that’s pinMode(6, OUTPUT) and digitalWrite(6, HIGH) after wiringPiSetup(). Its official GitHub repository has the source and the Python version.

Note: The Orange Pi Zero 3’s GPIOs are not 5v tolerant: the H616 datasheet allows no more than VCC + 0.3v (about 3.6v) on a pin. 5v on a header pin can damage the H618. To connect 5v logic (an Arduino’s TX pin, a 5v sensor output), use a level shifter or a resistor divider.

Orange Pi Zero 3 Boot Strap Pins and Pin 7

Pins 11 (PC6) and 13 (PC5) of the Orange Pi Zero 3 are the H618’s BOOT_SEL4 and BOOT_SEL3 boot-select straps. The board holds pin 11 high with a 10kΩ pull-up and pin 13 low with a 10kΩ pull-down, and the H618 reads them at power-up to decide where to boot from.

Once Linux is running, both are normal GPIOs. The catch is at power-up: if something you’ve wired holds pin 11 low or pin 13 high while the board starts, it may not boot from the SPI flash and microSD card as usual. Keep whatever drives these pins high-impedance at power-up (a sensor output that only switches later is fine), or use the other free GPIOs (12, 15, 22, 26) first.

Pin 7 (PC9) is the manual’s GPIO test pin, but the schematic also ties it to the AXP313A PMU’s IRQ output through R42 (0Ω). Orange Pi doesn’t say whether R42 is fitted on production boards. Treat pin 7 as an input, or check that R42 isn’t fitted before you drive it, so your output never fights the PMU.

Orange Pi Zero 3 Analog Input Pins (ADC)

The Orange Pi Zero 3 has no analog input pins on either header: none of the H618 pins brought out to the headers has an ADC function. To read a potentiometer or an analog sensor, add an external ADC such as an ADS1115 (16-bit, 4 channels, I2C) on I2C3 (pins 3 and 5), or an MCP3008 (10-bit, SPI) on SPI1. Power the ADC from 3.3v, so its digital lines stay at the board’s logic level.

Orange Pi Zero 3 UART Pins

The Orange Pi Zero 3’s main UART on the 26-pin header is UART5, on pin 8 (TX) and pin 10 (RX). It’s off by default: enable ph-uart5 on Orange Pi OS and it appears as /dev/ttyS5 ( /dev/ttyAS5 on Linux 5.4 images).

Orange Pi Zero 3 Default UART Pins: PH2 (UART5 TX, pin 8) and PH3 (UART5 RX, pin 10).

UART2 also has RTS/CTS on pins 19 and 21, but it sits on the I2C3 and SPI1 pins, so UART5 is the one to use. Cross the lines to your device (board TX to device RX), connect GND, and keep the device at 3.3v levels. Test it with a jumper from pin 8 to pin 10 and the manual’s gpio serial /dev/ttyS5 loopback.

There are so many modules that we can interface with the Orange Pi Zero 3 using the UART, which include: GPS receivers, 3D-printer controller boards (Klipper), RS-485 transceivers (Modbus), LoRa and cellular modems, fingerprint sensors, microcontroller boards, and much more.

Orange Pi Zero 3 I2C Pins

The Orange Pi Zero 3’s main I2C bus is I2C3 (Allwinner calls it TWI3), on pin 3 (SDA) and pin 5 (SCL), the same position as the main I2C bus on a Raspberry Pi. Both pins already have 2kΩ pull-ups on the board. Enable ph-i2c3 on Orange Pi OS and it appears as /dev/i2c-3.

Orange Pi Zero 3 Default I2C Pins: PH5 (I2C3 SDA, pin 3) and PH4 (I2C3 SCL, pin 5).

The bus number is the usual surprise: Orange Pi OS calls the bus /dev/i2c-3, but Armbian and other mainline-kernel images number it /dev/i2c-2, so a sensor library hard-coded for bus 3 finds nothing. Run ls /dev/i2c-* and sudo i2cdetect -y <bus> to see which number your image uses. Most sensor modules carry their own pull-ups too; that’s fine, they just sit in parallel with the board’s 2kΩ.

There are so many modules and sensors that we can interface with the Orange Pi Zero 3 using the I2C bus, which include: RTC clocks (DS3231), OLED displays (SSD1306), temperature and humidity sensors (BME280, SHT31), IMUs (MPU6050), ADCs (ADS1115) for analog inputs, GPIO expanders (MCP23017), PWM drivers (PCA9685), and much more.

Orange Pi Zero 3 SPI Pins

The Orange Pi Zero 3 has 1 SPI bus on its header: SPI1 on pins 19 (MOSI), 21 (MISO) and 23 (CLK), with chip select 1 on pin 24. Enable spi1-cs1-spidev and it appears as /dev/spidev1.1.

Orange Pi Zero 3 Default SPI1 Pins: PH7 (MOSI, pin 19), PH8 (MISO, pin 21), PH6 (CLK, pin 23) and PH9 (CS1, pin 24).

The manual’s pin table calls pin 24 “SPI1_CS”, but the schematic’s ball name is SPI1_CS1, and Orange Pi’s own overlay ( spi1-cs1-spidev) agrees, which is why the node is spidev1.1. Chip select 0 exists too, but it’s on pin 3, shared with I2C3 SDA, so you can’t use I2C3 and SPI1 CS0 at the same time. For a second SPI device, use any free GPIO as a chip select. Test the bus with a jumper from pin 19 to pin 21 and sudo spidev_test -v -D /dev/spidev1.1.

Pin 22 (PC7) shows SPI0_CS1 in the diagram, but SPI0 is the bus of the on-board 16MB flash, and its other pins aren’t on the header. Use pin 22 as a GPIO.

There are so many modules that we can interface with the Orange Pi Zero 3 using the SPI bus, which include: TFT and e-paper displays, MCP3008 ADCs, MCP2515 CAN controllers, LoRa radios (SX127x), RFID readers (RC522), accelerometers for 3D-printer input shaping (ADXL345), and much more.

Orange Pi Zero 3 PWM Pins

The Orange Pi Zero 3 has 2 hardware PWM pins on its 26-pin header: PWM1 on pin 10 (PH3) and PWM2 on pin 8 (PH2). Two more, PWM3 and PWM4, sit on the debug UART’s TX and RX pins, and you only get them if you give up the serial console.

With ph-pwm12 enabled (and ph-uart5 off), the manual drives PWM1 through sysfs on pwmchip0. This gives a 50Hz signal with a 1ms pulse on pin 10, the signal a servo wants:

1
2
3
4
echo 1 > /sys/class/pwm/pwmchip0/export
echo 20000000 > /sys/class/pwm/pwmchip0/pwm1/period       # 20ms = 50Hz
echo 1000000 > /sys/class/pwm/pwmchip0/pwm1/duty_cycle    # 1ms
echo 1 > /sys/class/pwm/pwmchip0/pwm1/enable

With wiringOP, gpio mode 4 pwm (PWM1) or gpio mode 3 pwm (PWM2) starts a 23475Hz, 50% square wave, and gpio pwm, gpio pwmr, gpio pwmc and gpio pwmTone change the duty cycle and frequency. The catch: the wiringOP that came preinstalled on the early images (v1.0.2) can’t do hardware PWM. Build the latest wiringOP from Orange Pi’s GitHub first (the next branch).

Note: Never power a fan, a motor, or a buzzer straight from a PWM pin. A GPIO can only switch a logic signal (6mA max on the H616). Drive the load through a MOSFET or a motor driver, and power it from the 5v pins.

There are so many things that we can control with the Orange Pi Zero 3 PWM pins, which include: LED dimming (through a transistor), DC motor speed (through a driver), servo motors, fan speed on a 4-pin PWM fan, buzzers and tone generation, and much more.

Orange Pi Zero 3 Debug UART Pins

The Orange Pi Zero 3’s Linux console is on a separate 3-pin debug UART header at the board edge, next to the 26-pin header. It’s the H618’s UART0 (PH0/PH1) at 3.3v levels, and it runs at 115200 baud, 8N1.

You need a 3.3v USB-to-TTL adapter (CP2102, CH340, FT232R): there’s no USB serial on the board. Open it with picocom -b 115200 /dev/ttyUSB0 on Linux, or PuTTY at 115200 on Windows. It shows the bootloader and kernel messages, which is the fastest way to see why an overlay failed or why the board won’t boot. It’s the kernel console, so it isn’t meant for talking to a 3D printer’s controller board or another microcontroller: use UART5 on pins 8 and 10 for that.

Orange Pi Zero 3 On-Board Features

Besides its two headers, the Orange Pi Zero 3 has a few on-board features you’ll meet when you wire and debug it. There are no buttons at all: no reset, no power, and no boot key, so you power-cycle the board to reset it.

Power a new board with no microSD card and both LEDs light up: that’s the factory mini Linux in the SPI flash, a quick way to check the board works before you flash a card. To turn the green LED into an “I’m alive” light for your own program, set its trigger to none in /sys/class/leds/ and write 1 or 0 to its brightness.

Getting Started With Orange Pi Zero 3 GPIO

Getting the Orange Pi Zero 3’s pins working takes three steps: install an OS, enable the overlays you need, and pick a GPIO library.

  1. Install an OS: flash Orange Pi OS (Debian or Ubuntu based), Armbian, DietPi, or another image to a microSD card. Orange Pi’s own images ship wiringOP and the overlay names used in this guide, so they’re the easiest start for GPIO work.
  2. Enable the interfaces: on Orange Pi OS, run sudo orangepi-config → System → Hardware, tick the overlays, then save and reboot. It writes them to the overlays= line of /boot/orangepiEnv.txt. On Armbian, use armbian-config or the overlays= line of /boot/armbianEnv.txt.
  3. Pick a library: libgpiod for C/C++ and Python (gpiochip0 + Linux number), wiringOP for Arduino-style C code and gpio readall, or wiringOP-Python. Use the Linux device nodes ( /dev/ttyS*, /dev/i2c-*, /dev/spidev1.1, /sys/class/pwm) for the buses.

On Armbian: the overlay names have changed between releases, so a name copied from an old guide (an -ph style name that no longer exists, for example) can fail without a clear message. Users report this most often with the UART overlays. List the names your image really ships with ls /boot/dtb/allwinner/overlay/, and check the boot log on the debug UART if a device node doesn’t appear.

Note: Some overlays share pins, and only one of each pair can work: ph-uart5 vs ph-pwm12 (pins 8/10, also I2C2), I2C3 vs SPI1 CS0 and UART2 (pin 3), and SPI1 vs I2C4 and UART2 (pins 19/21/23). After an overlay claims its pins, don’t use them as plain GPIOs.

Orange Pi Zero 3 Common Problems & Fixes

Why is there no /dev/i2c-3 on Armbian? Armbian and other mainline-kernel images number the 26-pin I2C3 bus /dev/i2c-2 (overlay i2c3-ph), and UART5 /dev/ttyS1. Orange Pi OS uses /dev/i2c-3 and /dev/ttyS5. Check ls /dev/i2c-* /dev/ttyS* and use the number your image gives you.

Why doesn’t hardware PWM work? Three usual causes: the wiringOP preinstalled on early images (v1.0.2) can’t do hardware PWM, so build the latest one from Orange Pi’s GitHub; ph-uart5 is still enabled and owns pins 8/10, so turn it off and enable ph-pwm12; or, on Armbian, no PWM overlay is enabled, so /sys/class/pwm is empty.

Why do pins 16 and 18 (or 3 and 5) always read 1? They have 2kΩ pull-ups on the board, so they idle high and a pull-down setting can’t change that. Use them as active-low inputs (a button to GND), or pick pins 12, 15, 22, or 26 when you need a pin that can be pulled down.

Can I connect my 3D printer’s controller board to the debug UART? No. The 3-pin header is the Linux console, and kernel messages would land on your printer’s serial line. Enable ph-uart5 and use pins 8 (TX) and 10 (RX) instead.

The board keeps restarting during boot, or gets stuck on startup. Why? Orange Pi’s manual blames most of these on the power supply: use a 5v / 2A or 3A supply and a short, good USB-C cable. A worn or slow microSD card is the next suspect. The debug UART shows exactly where the boot stops.

My overlay doesn’t show up in /dev. What’s wrong? Check the spelling on the single overlays= line, that two overlays don’t claim the same pin (UART5 and PWM1/PWM2, for example), that you used the overlay name of your OS (Orange Pi OS and Armbian differ), and reboot.

Will a 5v sensor or relay module work on the GPIOs? Not directly. The pins are 3.3v only and aren’t 5v tolerant. Use a level shifter for 5v signals, and a transistor or a relay module with its own driver for loads.

Orange Pi Zero 3 vs Orange Pi Zero 2W, Orange Pi Zero and Raspberry Pi Zero 2 W

The Orange Pi Zero 3 and the Orange Pi Zero 2W share the same H618 chip, but the Zero 2W swaps the 26-pin header and Ethernet for a Raspberry Pi Zero-shaped board with a full 40-pin header. The original Orange Pi Zero has the same 26-pin + 13-pin layout as the Zero 3 with a much older chip. Don’t confuse any of them with the new Orange Pi Zero 3W, a different board built around the Allwinner A733.

Pick the Orange Pi Zero 3 when you want Gigabit Ethernet and up to 4GB of RAM in a tiny board for a server, a 3D-printer host, or a network gadget, and 17 GPIOs are enough. Pick the Orange Pi Zero 2W when you need a 40-pin header and the Pi Zero shape, and the Raspberry Pi Zero 2 W for the best software support and HAT ecosystem. The original Orange Pi Zero only makes sense if you already have one.

Wrap Up

To conclude this guide article, the Orange Pi Zero 3 is a very solid little single-board computer when you need Gigabit Ethernet, Wi-Fi 5, and up to 4GB of RAM in a 50 x 55mm board, with a compact 26-pin header for your hardware: 17 GPIOs, I2C3, UART5, SPI1, and two hardware PWM pins, plus four more GPIOs on the 13-pin header. Just remember the gotchas: every pin is 3.3v only, pins 3, 5, 16, and 18 idle high because of their 2kΩ pull-ups, pins 11 and 13 are boot straps, and the I2C and UART device names change between Orange Pi OS and Armbian.
